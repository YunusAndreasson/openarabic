I said: Scholars and virtuous people have always delighted in witticisms and brightened at them, for they refresh the soul and relieve the heart from the strain of thought. Shu'ba used to narrate hadith, and whenever he saw al-Mazid the grammarian, he would say: "It is Abu Zayd al-Basit: 'The abode of Nu'm has gone silent, speaking nothing to us — though the abode, had it spoken, would have much to tell.'" We have transmitted from Ibn 'A'isha several witty anecdotes, some of which contain coarse humor. A man once said to him: "Does someone like you really say such things?" He replied: "Woe to you — do you not see their chains of transmission? Not one of those I narrate from is anything less than superior to all the people of our age. But you are among those whose inner selves are ugly, so they see only the outward appearance, whereas the inner life of those people was far above their outward manner."

A man among the ascetics was described in the presence of 'Ubaydullah ibn 'A'isha, and they said: "He is all seriousness." He replied: "He has narrowed his own pasture and cut short his range of vision. If he freed himself by shifting from one state to another, it would relieve the tightness of that knot, and he would return to seriousness with renewed energy and sharpness."

Al-Asma'i reported: I heard al-Rashid say: "Witty anecdotes sharpen minds and open ears."

It is reported that Hammad ibn Salama used to say: "Only the manliest of men love witticisms, and only the effeminate among them dislike them."

Al-Asma'i said: I recited to Muhammad ibn 'Imran al-Tamimi, the judge of Medina — and I never saw a judge more intelligent than him — the following verses: "O you who ask about my lodging — I have put up at the inn at my own expense. Bread comes to me from a baker who accepts neither collateral nor credit. I eat from my own purse and my own clothing, until even my teeth ache." He said: "Write it down for me." I said: "May God grant you well-being — only young men copy down such things." He replied: "Woe to you, write it down! For people of distinction delight in clever humor."

So it is clear from what we have mentioned that the souls of scholars roam freely in permissible amusement — the kind that renews their vigor for serious work, as though they had never left seriousness at all. Abu Firas said: "I ease the heart with a bit of jest — a feigned ignorance, though I am not ignorant. I joke as people of merit joke, for humor is sometimes the polish of the mind."

If someone were to say: "Telling stories of fools and simpletons provokes laughter, and you have narrated from the Prophet ﷺ that he said: 'A man may utter a word to make his companions laugh, and by it he plummets farther than the Pleiades'" — the answer is: this refers to someone who makes people laugh through lying. Indeed, it has been narrated explicitly in another version of the hadith: "Woe to the one who tells people things and lies in order to make them laugh."

It is permissible for a person to intend to make someone laugh on occasion. In Sahih Muslim, it is reported from 'Umar ibn al-Khattab that he said: "Let me speak to the Messenger of God — perhaps I can make him laugh." He said: "I told him: 'If you had seen the daughter of Zayd, the wife of 'Umar, demanding money from me — I struck her on the neck!'" And the Messenger of God ﷺ laughed.

What is disliked is for a man to make a habit of amusing people, because laughter in small measure is not blameworthy — the Messenger ﷺ himself used to laugh until his back teeth showed. Rather, excess is what is disliked, as he said: "Too much laughter kills the heart." Finding pleasure in such things from time to time is like salt in the pot.

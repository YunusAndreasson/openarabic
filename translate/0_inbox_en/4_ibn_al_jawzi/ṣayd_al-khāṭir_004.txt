He says in *Sayd al-Khatir*: "I speak of my own condition — I never have my fill of reading books. Whenever I come across a book I haven't seen before, it is as though I have stumbled upon a treasure." He had examined the catalog of endowed books at the Nizamiyya School and found it contained six thousand volumes. He also went through the catalogs of Abu Hanifa's books, al-Humaydi's books, and the books of his teachers Abd al-Wahhab, Ibn Nasir, and Abu Muhammad al-Shikhab — which amounted to entire loads — along with every other book he could get his hands on. "If I said I have read through twenty thousand volumes," he wrote, "that would still be an understatement, and I am still seeking more." Through this reading, he gained insight into the lives of the great scholars, the magnitude of their ambitions, the strength of their memory, the depth of their worship, and the wonders of their knowledge — things unknown to anyone who has not read as widely. "It made me look down on what most people occupy themselves with," he said, "and regard the ambitions of ordinary students as trivial. Praise be to God."

Among what he read were many volumes of *Kitab al-Funun* by Ibn Aqil — an enormous work rich in benefits across preaching, Quranic exegesis, jurisprudence, the two foundations of Islamic law, grammar, language, poetry, history, and anecdotes. It also contained Ibn Aqil's debates, his teaching sessions, his reflections, and the fruits of his thinking, all recorded within it. Ibn al-Jawzi drew immense benefit from it, and it was likely this book that inspired him to record his own reflections — which became his book *Sayd al-Khatir*. In sum, *Kitab al-Funun* left a clear mark on Ibn al-Jawzi's scholarship and writings. He said of it, may God have mercy on him: "This book comprises two hundred volumes, of which I obtained about one hundred and fifty."

His jealousy over the hours of his life was intense — he would not let any time go to waste without benefit. Even the brief moments he was obliged to spend receiving visitors, he would fill with tasks related to his scholarly work. He says in *Sayd al-Khatir*: "I prepared tasks that do not interfere with conversation for the times when I receive guests, so that no time passes empty. As preparation for their visits, I would cut paper, sharpen pens, and bind notebooks — things that must be done anyway and require neither deep thought nor full attention. I reserved them for the hours of their visits, so that nothing of my time would be lost."

Ibn al-Jawzi then concluded that writing books on preaching would complement his public sermons — indeed, it would extend their benefit more widely. A book reaches where a speech cannot, and it endures long after its author lies buried beneath the earth. He said, may God have mercy on him: "I came to the sound opinion that the benefit of writing books exceeds the benefit of teaching face to face — for in my lifetime I can only address a limited number of students in person, whereas through my writings I address countless people not yet born. The proof of this is that people benefit more from the works of earlier scholars than from what they learn directly from their own teachers. A scholar, therefore, should devote himself to writing, if God grants him the ability to produce works of genuine benefit."

Ibn al-Jawzi began writing at the age of seventeen. It is no wonder, then, that he became one of the most prolific authors in the history of Islam, with his works exceeding four hundred books.

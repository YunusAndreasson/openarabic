Ibn al-Jawzi was elegant in appearance, refined and clean, and lived comfortably. He was, may God have mercy on him, a brilliant speaker. If he wanted to make people laugh, he could make even a grieving mother laugh with his tales of misers, fools, simpletons, jesters, and wits. And if he wanted to make people weep, he could make even deaf stone cry with his descriptions of the Hereafter, the horrors of the Day of Judgment, and the lives of righteous ascetics and their fear of God Almighty. He would blend the two to captivate hearts and put souls at ease, preparing them to receive his sermons — for he was an expert in what mends and wins over souls. In addition to his eloquence and vast memorization, Ibn al-Jawzi was known for his quick wit and ready answers. Many amusing stories are told about him, including this one: a man asked him who the best person was after the Prophet, peace be upon him — at a time when tensions between the Shia and the Sunnis ran high. He replied without hesitation: "The best of people is the one whose daughter was under him." The Sunnis said: he means Abu Bakr, because his daughter Aisha was married to the Prophet, peace be upon him. The Shia said: no, he means Ali, because the Prophet's daughter Fatima, may God be pleased with her, was married to him.

He married twice, may God have mercy on him. From his first wife he had ten children — five sons and five daughters. Four of his sons died, among them his son Abd al-Aziz, who was poisoned in Mosul. Only Ali Abu al-Qasim survived, for whom he wrote the treatise *Laftat al-Kabad ila Nasihat al-Walad* — yet the son did not benefit from his father's counsel. He was disobedient and ill-natured, and when his father fell into hardship, he seized his books and sold them for next to nothing. As for his daughter Rabi'a, she married the son of Rashid al-Tabari, who became absorbed in the books of the philosophers and whose beliefs went astray, so Ibn al-Jawzi cut ties with him. When he died, Ibn al-Jawzi married her to a Turkish mamluk of the vizier Ibn Hubayra named Qizghali. She bore Yusuf, known as Sibt Ibn al-Jawzi, the author of *Mir'at al-Zaman* and other works.

From his second wife he had his son Yusuf, the youngest and most distinguished of his children. He preached after his father, wrote prolifically, and mastered his craft until he surpassed his peers. He took up teaching at the Mustansiriyya school, built the Jawziyya school in Damascus and endowed it, and then became the chief tutor at the court of Caliph al-Musta'sim — until he was killed along with the caliph, together with his sons Taj al-Din, Jamal al-Din, and Sharaf al-Din, at the hands of the Mongols.

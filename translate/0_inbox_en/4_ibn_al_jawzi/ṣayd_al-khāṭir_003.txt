In his old age, Ibn al-Jawzi endured a harsh ordeal brought about by the Rafida. Sheikh Ali al-Tantawi summarized it in his introduction to the edition of *Sayd al-Khatir*, saying:

The Hanbali vizier Ibn Yunus had convened a tribunal for Rukn Abd al-Salam ibn Abd al-Qadir al-Jili, and his books were burned — they contained a great deal of heresy, star worship, and the doctrines of the ancients. This took place in the presence of Ibn al-Jawzi and other scholars, and the vizier seized his grandfather's school and handed it over to Ibn al-Jawzi.

When Ibn al-Qassab, a malicious Rafidi, assumed the vizierate, he moved to arrest Ibn Yunus and pursued his associates. Rukn said to him, "What about Ibn al-Jawzi? He is a Nasibi and a descendant of Abu Bakr al-Siddiq. He is one of Ibn Yunus's closest allies — he gave him my grandfather's school, and my books were burned on his advice." Ibn al-Qassab wrote to the Caliph al-Nasir, who had Shi'ite sympathies and had, in his later years, grown cold toward Sheikh Abu al-Faraj. The Caliph ordered that Ibn al-Jawzi be handed over to Rukn Abd al-Salam.

Rukn came to the Sheikh's house, hurled insults at him, and treated him with the utmost harshness. He sealed his books and his home and scattered his family. When night fell, Ibn al-Jawzi was carried away in a boat with no one but his enemy Rukn for company. The Sheikh wore nothing but a thin undergarment with no trousers, and a light cap on his head. He was taken downriver to Wasit, whose governor was a Shi'ite. Rukn said to him, "Let me have my enemy so I can throw him in the dungeon." But the governor rebuked him, saying, "You heretic! You want me to throw him in on your word alone? Bring me a written order from the Caliph. By God, even if he were not of my own school, I would spend my life and wealth in his service."

When the case was heard in Wasit, Rukn ibn Abd al-Qadir gathered people and accused the Sheikh of misappropriating the school's endowment funds, claiming he had taken such-and-such amount — all of it lies. The Sheikh denied the charges and was truthful and upright. A house was set aside for him on Darb al-Diwan, with a guard at the door and a servant assigned to him. Some people would visit, listen to him, and take down his dictations, and he would send many poems to Baghdad.

He remained in Wasit for five years, serving himself — washing his own clothes, cooking his own food, and drawing water from the well — unable to leave even to go to a bathhouse, and he was nearly eighty years old.

The difference between these terms depends on the person's inner state and their conduct with others. If someone restrains themselves and resists the pull of what is unbecoming — and this restraint comes naturally as a settled trait — it is called *sabr* (patience). But if it comes through effort, practice, and swallowing its bitterness, it is called *tasabbur* (forcing oneself to be patient), as this grammatical form in Arabic denotes deliberate effort, like forcing oneself to be forbearing, courageous, generous, or tolerant.

When a person keeps practicing this effort and calling upon it, it eventually becomes second nature, as the Prophet said: "Whoever strives to be patient, God will grant him patience." Likewise, a person may force themselves to be chaste until chastity becomes second nature. The same applies to all character traits.

This is a matter people have disagreed about: can virtues truly be acquired, or does adopted behavior never become genuine character? As the poet said: "The heart is asked to forget you, but nature defies the one who tries to change it." And another said: "O you who adorns yourself with qualities not your own — affectation falls short of true character, and the ugliness of pretense is the mark of the pretender." Those who hold this view say that God has already determined creation, character, sustenance, and lifespan.

Another group, however, maintains that character can indeed be acquired, just as reason, forbearance, generosity, liberality, and courage can be acquired — and reality bears witness to this. They say: repeated practice produces settled dispositions. What this means is that whoever keeps doing something, grows accustomed to it, and trains themselves in it, it becomes a fixed trait, a second nature, and an ingrained quality. They say: habits transform natures. A person keeps forcing himself to be patient until patience becomes his nature, just as he keeps forcing himself toward forbearance, dignity, composure, and steadfastness until these become his character traits, as deeply rooted as inborn qualities. They say: God has placed in the human being the capacity to receive and to learn, so transforming natural dispositions is not impossible.

However, this transformation may be weak, in which case the person reverts to his original nature at the slightest provocation. It may be strong but not yet deeply rooted, so the person may still revert when the provocation is powerful and intense. And it may become so firmly established that the person develops what amounts to a second nature — in which case he hardly ever reverts to the disposition he left behind.

As for *istibar*, it goes further than *tasabbur*. It is an active striving for patience, standing in relation to patience as earning stands in relation to acquisition. *Tasabbur* is the beginning of *istibar*, just as initial effort is the precursor to full acquisition. A person keeps practicing *tasabbur* until it matures into *istibar*.

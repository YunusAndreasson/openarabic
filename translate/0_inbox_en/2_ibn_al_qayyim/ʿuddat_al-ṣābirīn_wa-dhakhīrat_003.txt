It is said that patience is the courage of the soul, and from this the saying was derived: "Courage is but an hour of patience." It is also said that patience is the steadfastness of the heart in the face of turmoil. Patience and panic are opposites, each standing against the other. God said of the people of Hell: *"Whether we panic or endure, it is all the same for us — there is no escape."* Panic is the companion and twin of helplessness, while patience is the companion and substance of shrewdness. If panic were asked, "Who is your father?" it would answer, "Helplessness." And if shrewdness were asked the same, it would answer, "Patience."

The soul is the mount upon which a person rides toward either Paradise or the Fire, and patience is to the soul what a halter and reins are to a mount. Without halter or reins, the mount will bolt in every direction. It is preserved from the sermons of al-Hajjaj: "Restrain these souls, for they are ever inclined toward evil. May God have mercy on the person who places a halter and reins on his soul — leading it by its halter toward obedience to God and turning it by its reins away from disobedience to God. For enduring what God has forbidden is easier than enduring His punishment."

I say: the soul possesses two forces — the force of initiative and the force of restraint. The true nature of patience is to direct the force of initiative toward what benefits a person and to use the force of restraint to hold back from what harms him. Among people, some have greater patience for doing what benefits them and persisting in it than for abstaining from what harms them — they endure the hardship of worship but cannot resist the call of desire toward what is forbidden. Others have greater patience in avoiding transgressions than in bearing the hardship of obedience. And some have no patience for either. The best of people are those most patient in both.

Many people endure the struggle of standing in night prayer through heat and cold, and bear the hardship of fasting, yet cannot resist a forbidden glance. Many can resist looking and turning toward alluring images, yet have no patience for commanding good and forbidding evil, or striving against disbelievers and hypocrites — they are the weakest and most helpless in that regard. Most people lack patience for either of the two, and the fewest among them are those steadfast in both.

It is said that patience is the persistence of the impulse of reason and faith against the impulse of desire and appetite. What this means is that human nature demands what it craves, while the impulse of reason and faith forbids it. The war between them rages on, swinging back and forth, and the battlefield of this war is the servant's heart — and patience, courage, and steadfastness are its weapons.

Since the one who seeks the straight path is seeking something that most people have turned away from — desiring to walk a road where fellow travelers are exceedingly few and rare — and since human souls are naturally inclined to feel lonely in solitude and to find comfort in companionship, God, glorified be He, drew attention to the companions on this path. They are those whom God has blessed: the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous — and what excellent companions they are. He attributed the path to those blessed travelers who walk it, so that the seeker of guidance would no longer feel the loneliness of standing apart from the people of his time and his kind, and so that he would know that his companions on this path are those whom God has favored. Thus he should pay no mind to those who have strayed from it and oppose him, for they are the lesser in worth, even if they are the greater in number.

As one of the early scholars said: "Hold fast to the path of truth, and do not be troubled by the fewness of those who walk it. Beware the path of falsehood, and do not be deceived by the multitude of those who perish upon it. Whenever you feel lonely in your solitude, look to the companions who have gone before you and strive to catch up with them. Turn your gaze away from all others, for they will avail you nothing before God. And if they call out to you along your way, do not turn toward them — for the moment you turn, they will seize you and hold you back."

I have given two parables for this, so keep them in mind.

The first parable: A man leaves his house heading to prayer, wanting nothing else. Along the way, a human devil confronts him, hurling hurtful words at him. The man stops and responds, and they become locked in struggle. Perhaps the human devil overpowers him and prevents him from reaching the mosque until the prayer has passed. Or perhaps the man is stronger, but his scuffle keeps him from the front row and from fully joining the congregation. If he turns his attention to his adversary, he emboldens him, and his resolve may weaken. But if the man has knowledge and understanding, he quickens his pace to make up for his delay — or more. And if he simply ignores his enemy and focuses on his purpose, fearing he will miss the prayer or its time, then his enemy will not get what he wanted from him.

The second parable: The gazelle runs faster than the dog, but when it senses the dog and turns to look back, its pace slackens, and the dog catches it.

The point is this: mentioning these companions removes the loneliness of standing alone, and it urges the traveler to press on and hasten to join them. This is also one of the wisdoms in the supplication of qunut: "O God, guide me among those whom You have guided" — meaning: include me in this company, and make me a companion among them and with them.

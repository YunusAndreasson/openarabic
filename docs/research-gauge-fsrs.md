# Scientific Rationale for a Red/Yellow/Green FSRS Practice System

## Memory Load & Cognitive Capacity

Spaced repetition research emphasizes that trying to review too many items at once can overload the learner’s cognitive capacity. Working memory can only actively hold a few items at a time, so a large backlog of due words increases mental effort and fatigue. Spaced practice naturally mitigates this by spreading reviews out, which **reduces cognitive load** and prevents overwhelming the brain. In fact, spacing out reviews helps avoid **memory interference** – the confusion of similar memories – by giving time to distinguish and consolidate items. The FSRS algorithm even includes features to combat interference: for example, it can **disperse “sibling” cards** (related cards due around the same time) to different days, explicitly noting that this _“alleviates the interference”_ and improves efficiency.

From a cognitive standpoint, **prioritizing due words (urgent reviews)** makes sense. These are items at the threshold of being forgotten – reviewing them now prevents memory decay from exceeding capacity. There is evidence that memories are strengthened most when retrieval is difficult but _successful_ – essentially when an item is reviewed _right as it’s about to be forgotten_. Practicing due words on time leverages this principle: recall requires more effort at that point, which yields a bigger long-term retention gain (a classic “desirable difficulty” effect). In contrast, mixing in too many not-yet-due or already well-known items (while neglecting truly due ones) could waste time on easy recalls and allow fragile memories to slip. Thus, **doing due reviews first** aligns with both FSRS’s design and cognitive science: it keeps the learner’s memory load optimal and prevents high-priority items from falling out of the 90% recall zone the scheduler aims to maintain. In summary, minimizing backlog and tackling due cards promptly helps avoid overload and interference, ensuring one’s limited cognitive capacity is used efficiently on material at risk of forgetting.

## Struggling Words & Difficulty Metrics

FSRS assigns each card a _difficulty_ score (D) which adjusts based on performance – hard failures make D approach 10 (maximum), while easy successes decrease it. **High difficulty cards (e.g. D ≥ 8.0)** indicate a learner is consistently struggling with those words, often due to multiple lapses. Cognitively, such items demand **greater mental effort** on each review. Research on the _retrieval effort hypothesis_ shows that **difficult-to-retrieve memories require more cognitive processing** (e.g. higher attentional and neural activation) than easy ones. In other words, when a word is very hard for you, each attempt to recall it taxes working memory and attention more strongly. This increased effort can be beneficial for learning (successful tough recalls produce large memory gains) but it also means **practicing unstable words is mentally taxing** in the moment. By contrast, _stable, easy words_ can be recalled with minimal effort, almost automatically. FSRS accounts for this by giving hard items only small interval increases – _“the larger the value of D, the smaller the stability increment”_ on each success – effectively acknowledging that **difficult material doesn’t “stick” as easily** and needs more frequent review.

There is evidence that focusing on high-difficulty (or “unstable”) items engages more intense cognitive processes. Psychologically, this falls under **“desirable difficulties”** – conditions that are challenging (e.g. barely successful recalls) tend to enhance long-term retention. However, if an item is _too_ difficult (e.g. answered incorrectly repeatedly), it can cross into “undesirable difficulty,” where the learner’s resources may be overtaxed with little benefit. In practice, FSRS and other SRS tools often flag such leeches (e.g. Anki’s leech threshold) to be handled differently (rephrase the card, study it in a simpler way, or temporarily suspend it). This is because **practicing only very struggling words can yield diminishing returns** – the high error rate can be discouraging and time-consuming. A balanced approach is to review tough words more (as the algorithm schedules), but also intermix them with easier material to manage mental fatigue. Notably, FSRS’s difficulty metric has a mean-reversion mechanism: if you start getting a card right (marking “Good” repeatedly), its difficulty will gradually drift back down toward a default value. This implies **difficulty is a dynamic, contextual measure**, not a fixed trait of the word – a high difficulty today can improve with practice. In summary, _struggling words require heavier cognitive lifting_, and the system acknowledges this by scheduling them more often and by encouraging the learner to give them special attention (or even a different strategy) compared to stable words. This ensures one’s mental energy is appropriately allocated to the toughest vocabulary, which aligns with cognitive research showing **greater benefits from effortful retrieval** – as long as that effort remains productive and not overwhelming.

## Memory Capacity Thresholds (70% / 40%)

The choice of **70% and 40% “memory capacity” thresholds** in the color-coding scheme can be related to findings in learning science, though these exact numbers are not fixed rules. In spaced repetition, a common metric is _expected retention_ (probability of recall). Many SRS algorithms (including FSRS) traditionally target around **90% recall** for scheduled reviews, balancing high retention with efficiency. However, some experimentation in the community has involved intentionally lower retention targets – for instance, setting desired retention to \~70% to reduce review workload. Users report that at _70% retention, each recall attempt is more effortful than at 90%, but potentially yields greater memory consolidation_. The logic is that allowing a bit more forgetting makes the reviews you do perform more challenging (increasing long-term durability of memories), albeit at the cost of more failures. In fact, one Anki forum user noted _“at 70% retention, each successful recall takes more cognitive effort than... at 90% retention. This means the value of that recall for durability... is even greater.”_. This aligns with the _desirable difficulty_ principle. That said, 70% is **near the lower bound of what many consider acceptable** for general use – it means 3 out of 10 cards will be forgotten when due, which can feel frustrating. Empirical research suggests an **optimal success rate** around _80–85%_ in practice testing for efficient learning – challenging enough to promote growth, but not so hard that most attempts fail. An 85% recall rate is often cited as a sweet spot that maximizes learning per time without overly demoralizing the learner. In that context, a 70% threshold is a bit aggressive (high difficulty, low safety margin) and a 40% threshold would be **very low**, likely tipping into diminishing returns with too many lapses.

If we interpret “memory capacity” as the proportion of learned material the user can recall at a given time, then **40% recall roughly corresponds to Ebbinghaus’s observations of un-reviewed learning after a few days**. Classic forgetting curve data shows that without review, memory retention drops steeply (often less than 50% after a day or two). Hitting a 40% retention level means the learner has forgotten more than half of what they knew – a clear “red zone” indicating overload or insufficient review. By contrast, **70% retention** (yellow zone) suggests moderate forgetting – some strain on memory, but not total failure. Cognitive science doesn’t prescribe these exact percentages as hard limits, but it does support the idea of **monitoring one’s recall rate to stay within a productive range**. For instance, if overall recall falls much below \~60%, the learner may be taking in new material too fast or not reviewing enough. FSRS’s own **“average retrievability”** metric is essentially a measure of what percent of your collection you’d recall at a given moment. Normally, if you aim for 90% on due cards, your average across all cards might be \~95% (since non-due cards are above the threshold). If you let reviews lapse, that average falls. So, one can think of 70% and 40% as custom cut-offs chosen to communicate urgency: _above \~70% overall retention (green) you’re in a safe zone_, but if your **estimated retention drops to \~40% (red)**, it means a large chunk of your vocabulary is likely forgotten or at high risk, and urgent review is needed. While not explicitly drawn from a specific paper, this scheme resonates with the known **trade-offs between review frequency and forgetting**: researchers have shown that **too high a retention goal (e.g. 95%+) yields an explosion of reviews, whereas too low (50–60%) means a lot of re-learning**. The chosen thresholds appear to balance these extremes, echoing the idea that _some_ forgetting is optimal, but beyond a certain point the costs outweigh the benefits. In summary, **70% vs 40% can be seen as markers of memory load**: \~70% (yellow) might indicate your memory is \~70% “full” and starting to strain, while \~40% (red) means it’s largely “emptying out” and you’re in danger of serious knowledge loss – hence aligning with cognitive capacity concerns and justifying urgent action.

## Practice Session Optimization

**How should practice sessions be composed?** The FSRS algorithm itself schedules each card optimally, but it doesn’t dictate _how_ a user groups their study sessions – that’s left to the interface and user preference. Nonetheless, certain best practices are implied by the science and the algorithm’s design:

- **Prioritize due reviews**: As discussed, cards should ideally be reviewed as soon as they become due (i.e. when their recall probability decays to the target threshold). Both Anki and FSRS follow this principle – due cards are queued for review each day and are usually addressed first before anything else. Completing due reviews on time maximizes retention and minimizes the need to re-learn forgotten items. If one has separate session types (e.g. a “Due words” session vs a “New words” session), doing the due/overdue items first is empirically supported. **Anki’s default behavior** is to show daily reviews (due cards) before introducing new cards, precisely to prevent backlog buildup and knowledge decay. This approach is backed by the **spacing effect**, which has a mountain of evidence: spacing out reviews (instead of cramming or skipping them) significantly improves long-term retention _and_ reduces total study time required. A meta-analysis found spaced repetition boosted retention by \~46% while cutting study time by \~35% compared to massed review. In short, an “All words” mixed session is useful, but a session focusing on due items is critical for maintaining learned vocabulary efficiently.

- **Target difficult items appropriately**: Having a “challenging words” session (focusing on high-difficulty or recently lapsed terms) can be beneficial in moderation. Modern adaptive learning research suggests that **adapting practice to item difficulty yields better outcomes than a one-size-fits-all schedule**. For example, one study showed that _adaptive spacing based on item learning strength led to greater gains than fixed intervals_. FSRS is an embodiment of this adaptive approach – it gives “struggling” cards shorter review intervals and higher scheduling priority. By offering a session mode specifically for such struggling words, the system lets the learner devote extra attention where needed. This is conceptually similar to how **teachers advise focusing revision on the hardest material** once basics are in place. There isn’t a specific FSRS paper prescribing a separate “hard items session,” but the algorithm’s difficulty metric and the notion of **“overdueness”** (how far a card is beyond its scheduled time) can be used to identify which cards are shaky. In Anki, for instance, users sometimes create filtered decks for “leeches” (cards failed multiple times) to review them intensively or decide if they need reformulation. The key is balance – research on **interleaving** indicates that mixing item types can improve discrimination and learning. Rather than doing an entire session of nothing but very hard cards (which can be mentally exhausting), **interleaving those hard items with other reviews** might improve overall retention and attentiveness. Interleaving practice of different topics or difficulty levels is considered a _desirable difficulty_ itself, forcing the brain to continuously adapt retrieval cues and thus enhancing learning compared to a blocked, uniform practice.

- **Optimize session composition for goals**: If the goal is **long-term retention**, spacing and proper scheduling are paramount – hence a due-review session (red, urgent) is aligned with that goal. If the goal is **remediation of weak areas**, a challenging-words session (yellow) makes sense to allocate extra practice to those items. And an **“all words” session (green)** – effectively a mixed or catch-up session – can be useful for general review or when the learner has surplus time and wants to reinforce everything. There is evidence that _over-practice_ (reviewing even stable items occasionally beyond the minimum) can still benefit memory, though with diminishing returns. The **testing effect** tells us any active recall, even of well-known items, strengthens memory to a degree. So an “All words” session that includes stable cards isn’t wasted effort, especially if it keeps the learner engaged. However, it’s less critical than the targeted sessions, which is why the design labels it green (low urgency). In line with cognitive load theory, it’s wise to **avoid mixing too many new learnings into a review session** as that can increase intrinsic load – thus many SRS separate new material introduction from review. If a user does combine them (in an “all” session), the interface’s color cues can help the user seamlessly navigate between reviewing an urgent due item and learning a new one without losing focus.

In summary, while specific “session types” aren’t mandated by the FSRS algorithm, they are a logical extension for interface design. They allow the learner to **triage their study time**: tackle time-sensitive due items first (prevent memory loss), concentrate on unstable items second (bolster weak memories), and then, if able, review everything or learn new items. This strategy echoes well-established principles: **review just at the point of need**, give extra practice to things you find hard, and occasionally **refresh older knowledge** to keep it alive. By aligning session choices with memory states, the system helps the learner optimize retention and efficiency, which is the very aim of spaced repetition.

In educational contexts, this metaphor has been explicitly applied to learning status. The reported benefit is twofold: it promotes metacognition (students become aware of their weak vs. strong areas at a glance) and it “provides direction to how they should study” next – essentially exactly what our FSRS color buttons aim to do for the user.

Studies in user experience note that using familiar visual cues greatly **reduces cognitive load** and speeds up decision-making. ## ts-FSRS Specific Implementation Details

The **TypeScript FSRS (ts-fsrs)** library implements the “Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler” algorithm, which is built on two key memory models: **stability (S)** and **difficulty (D)**. In FSRS, _memory stability_ represents how long an item can be retained – concretely, S is defined as the time (in days) it takes for a recently reviewed item’s recall probability (R) to fall from 100% down to 90%. In essence, a higher stability means the card’s content is more deeply ingrained and will be remembered for longer intervals. Each successful review increases a card’s S value (pushing the next interval further out), whereas a lapse resets or lowers stability. The algorithm’s formulas ensure **diminishing returns on stability**: when S is already high, making it even higher is harder (each subsequent review yields a smaller proportional increase). This reflects the cognitive reality that once a memory is very solid (say, you know a word for years), an additional review doesn’t lengthen its retention by as large a factor as when the memory was new. It’s essentially modeling a _saturation effect_ in memory durability.

_Difficulty (D)_ in FSRS is a number indicating how easy or hard that item is for the learner **in the long run**. Unlike stability, difficulty isn’t a physical quantity with a strict definition; the FSRS documentation calls it _“just a crude heuristic”_ without a precise unit. Initial difficulty is set based on the first learning session (rating “Easy” vs “Hard” etc.), and thereafter it **updates with each review**. The rule of thumb: if you press “Again” (forgot) or “Hard”, the difficulty goes up; if you press “Easy”, difficulty goes down; pressing “Good” leaves difficulty roughly unchanged (aside from a slow mean-reversion). The algorithm is designed such that D hovers between 1 and 10 and never quite reaches 10 – it approaches 10 asymptotically if a card continues to be forgotten many times. What does this mean cognitively? A _high difficulty score_ implies the card tends to be hard for you – maybe it’s abstract or easily confused, or you haven’t fully mastered it. A _low difficulty score_ means the card is generally easy for you (you recall it consistently without trouble). FSRS uses D to modulate the stability growth: **cards with higher D get much smaller interval increases** on successful reviews. Intuitively, if something is inherently difficult, even when you remember it once, the algorithm “trusts” that memory less – it schedules the next review sooner than it would for an easy card you got right. By contrast, easy items (low D) can have their intervals grow faster because the algorithm is confident you don’t need as frequent practice. Over time, difficulty in FSRS also has a gentle pull towards a default value (around 5) whenever you repeatedly get the card correct – this is the mean reversion (so a temporarily hard card can recover towards medium difficulty if you start doing well on it).

**Thresholds and categorization**: ts-fsrs itself doesn’t explicitly label cards as “red/yellow/green” – those are higher-level UI concepts. But it provides the data that enables such categorization. For example, one could map **retrievability R** (the current estimated recall probability) to a color: if R < 40%, that card is in the red zone (highly likely to be forgotten, effectively overdue), whereas if R is high (>90%), the card is safely in green. FSRS continuously updates each card’s retrievability in real time (it decays each day according to the card’s stability curve). In fact, FSRS can output an **“average retrievability”** for your whole collection, which, as mentioned, reflects your overall memory state (e.g. _“Today you should be able to recall X% of all your cards”_). A user interface could use this: if the average is dropping toward, say, 70%, it might turn some indicator yellow (meaning your memory is getting weaker, lots of cards due). FSRS itself defaults to a high retention goal (90%), but it’s tunable. There are no hard-coded “40% or 70%” thresholds built into FSRS; those are choices on how to interpret the data. However, FSRS’s author and community often discuss retention trade-offs – it’s understood, for instance, that if you set your target retention to 90%, cards become due at 90% recall, whereas at 80% target they’d be due later and you’d tolerate more forgetting. So, an app could reasonably decide that _if a user’s current recall probability is below \~50% on a chunk of cards, that’s a red-alert situation_. This is in line with Anki’s notion of “leeches,” which by default flags a card after 8 failures – essentially when a card’s difficulty stays extremely high and it’s been forgotten so often that standard scheduling isn’t working. FSRS doesn’t auto-suspend leeches, but it does track lapses and difficulty, so an integration could mark any card with D > 8 or >3 lapses as “struggling” (perhaps an orange label). These design decisions would be informed by FSRS data but are not dictated by the algorithm.

Notably, **ts-fsrs provides APIs to get all these metrics (S, D, R)** for each card, empowering the interface to create features like “Practice Hard Words” or show a stability/difficulty histogram. The _current version of FSRS (v6)_ even allows personalizing the forgetting curve with a per-user parameter (w20), which means the system models your memory more closely and can predict when a card hits, say, 40% recall for you. In the FSRS paper and documentation, there is an emphasis on optimizing _workload vs retention_ – e.g., finding the retention setting that yields minimal daily reviews for a given knowledge level. Those curves suggest there’s a point where making retention too high sharply increases workload. This supports using something like a **“yellow zone” (caution)** when the system estimates you’re approaching that workload tipping point. For instance, one might find that trying to keep >90% of all cards fresh is very taxing, whereas letting it slip to \~80% greatly eases load with modest knowledge loss. An app could communicate that by color: green (your memory load is light and efficient), yellow (you’re doing okay but starting to accumulate a lot to review), red (you’re now behind, many cards are slipping).

Finally, does ts-fsrs documentation give guidance on session prioritization? The official FSRS documentation mostly covers how the algorithm schedules cards and how to train the model on user data. It assumes the user will do all due cards in a reasonable timeframe. That said, the FSRS add-on for Anki includes features like **“Load Balance”** and **“Free Days”**, which automatically spread reviews to avoid any single day becoming overload. For example, the _Flatten_ feature (recently added to FSRS helper) will postpone some future reviews if a day is projected to have too many, preferentially delaying cards with high stability (easy ones). This is effectively the algorithm pre-emptively creating a _“green vs red” prioritization on a given day_: it leaves the most critical (low stability or overdue) cards for today and pushes out the rest. The FSRS dev noted that users like this flattening because it makes the due graph look manageable (perhaps a placebo), but it also has a real advantage of delaying _easy_ cards first so that delaying reviews has minimal impact on overall retention. This is very much in the spirit of what our color system does – it ensures the _urgent_ stuff is front and center. The documentation entry even says flattening by stability is preferable to Anki’s naive daily limit because _“if the review limit shifts low-stability (more fragile) cards to the future, the impact on retention might be greater”_. In other words, always do the hard/urgent ones now (red), and if you must delay, delay the stable ones (which would be green). Thus, while ts-fsrs doesn’t explicitly mention “color-coded buttons,” its tools and underlying logic implicitly endorse dividing cards by their stability/difficulty state and handling them accordingly.

## Spaced Repetition Best Practices in Scheduling

The proposed color-coding scheme aligns with broader best practices and findings in the spaced repetition literature:

- **Spacing and sequencing reviews**: Decades of research on the **spacing effect** confirm that information is retained far longer when reviews are spaced out optimally, rather than massed. An effective SRS (like FSRS) automatically computes these optimal intervals. However, learners still need to follow through by doing the reviews roughly on schedule. Best practice is to **maintain a regular review routine**, completing due items each day or session. Allowing reviews to pile up (skipping days) turns a spaced schedule into a backlogged, more massed one, which erodes the benefits. This is why Anki’s manual and seasoned users emphasize doing your due cards _every day_ if possible, or if not, using features like **“custom study – review overdue cards”** to catch up in manageable chunks. Our red zone is essentially highlighting that best practice: “These are overdue or due now – do them first.” By surfacing urgency, we encourage the user to honor the spacing schedule, which is crucial. As a cognitive rule: **reviewing an item right before it’s forgotten (but not after)** yields maximal reinforcement. Thus, scheduling algorithms aim for that, and user habits should too. The green “review all” option can complement this by allowing **extra review (over-review)** if desired, but standard practice suggests that systematically reviewing more than scheduled isn’t necessary for retention (it might be done for confidence or additional exposure, which is fine, but it’s less time-efficient than strictly following the spaced schedule).

- **Adaptive prioritization**: Modern SRS algorithms including SuperMemo’s SM-17/18 and FSRS represent a shift from fixed intervals (as in older SM-2, used by early Anki) to more adaptive, data-driven scheduling. Research by Wozniak (SuperMemo’s creator) and recent FSRS contributors (like Lin et al., who published on optimizing SRS with large datasets) all iterate on the idea that **items should be scheduled based on their individual difficulty and past performance**. A best practice here is _differentiating between easy vs hard items_. Anki already does this to an extent with its Ease factor (which FSRS replaces with the more sophisticated stability/difficulty model). In practical terms, this means **spend more time on difficult material** – not by brute force “cramming,” but by allocating appropriately frequent reviews and perhaps additional study techniques (mnemonics, context sentences, etc.) for those items. Our “challenging words” session embodies that principle: it offers a way to focus practice on the system-identified hard items. In educational psychology, _targeted practice_ on weak areas is often recommended (sometimes called _deliberate practice_ – focusing on the skills or items just outside one’s comfort zone). Our design does exactly this by segregating the hard items (orange zone). It’s also consistent with Vygotsky’s **Zone of Proximal Development** concept: the yellow zone words are those the learner can recall with some effort or hint (they’re in the learner’s proximal zone), so practicing them will likely yield improvement, whereas red might indicate items momentarily outside that zone (needing immediate review or they’ll fall out completely).

- **Interleaving and varied practice**: As mentioned, interleaving different topics or item types is generally beneficial for learning categories of problems (e.g. math or language contexts) because it improves transfer and discrimination. For simple fact recall like vocabulary, interleaving is less of an issue (the cards are anyway somewhat random in review). However, one could view our _“All words” (green) session_ as a form of **interleaved practice** – it doesn’t distinguish by difficulty or status, it just gives a mixed set of prompts. If a learner always and only did “red then stop,” they’d be doing a form of _blocked practice_ focusing on the most lagging items. While efficient, that might become monotonous or stress-inducing. Occasionally mixing in green sessions introduces variety (some easy, some hard, some new, some old) which can keep engagement up and exploit the benefits of interleaving to some extent. Cognitive science suggests that _mixing known and less-known items_ can enhance recall of the less-known items by providing contextual variety and preventing cues from only one context. In an SRS, this happens naturally to a degree, but allowing the user to do an all-inclusive review if they want can serve as a **self-testing tool**: the user can gauge overall how well they do when all cards (even “not due” ones) are thrown at them randomly – a bit like a mock exam. This kind of self-test can reinforce learning and provide insight (maybe they discover a “green” card wasn’t as solid as they thought, moving it mentally into yellow).

- **Avoiding overload and burnout**: A crucial practical consideration in SRS best practices is preventing user burnout. The best algorithm means little if the user gives up on using the system. This is echoed in the community; as one commenter noted, _“The real failure mode \[of SRS]... People give up because they get overwhelmed with reviews”_. Techniques to combat this include **setting daily limits, suspending or deleting leeches, and making cards easier**. Our design contributes to this by visually flagging when things are getting heavy (lots of red = you’re behind; perhaps you should stop adding new cards or even consider tweaking your load). It also encapsulates the _“make cards easier”_ advice: if a card is truly red repeatedly (keeps lapsing), maybe the user should edit it. The separate “challenging” queue might implicitly encourage them to notice patterns – e.g., if a particular subset of cards is always in the yellow/orange list, is there something about those (all abstract words? all lacking mnemonics?) that they can address. By giving difficult items a spotlight, we help ensure they’re not drowned in a sea of due cards or ignored. As HN users suggested, it’s wise not to let “rotten apples” pollute the bunch – in other words, _deal with your leeches_. Our system doesn’t automatically suspend them (that would be up to user judgment), but by isolating them in the challenging session, the user can more readily decide if an item needs extra work or rephrasing.

To sum up, the color-coded session approach is grounded in established SRS practices: **do reviews on time (spacing effect)**, **focus on hard items (adaptive learning, desirable difficulty)**, **mix practice for robust learning (interleaving, testing effect)**, and **be mindful of workload (avoid burnout by monitoring load)**. It complements FSRS by making its sophisticated scheduling more actionable and observable to the learner. By following the prompts (red→yellow→green), the learner is essentially following an optimal study strategy: **first preserve existing knowledge, then reinforce weak knowledge, then broaden/general review**. This mirrors the intuitive strategy a good tutor might use in a review session, and it aligns with cognitive science principles for effective learning and memory. Each color zone prompts the user to engage in a behavior that research endorses – whether it’s timely recall, effortful retrieval, or varied review – making the system not only algorithmically powerful, but also pedagogically sound and user-friendly.
